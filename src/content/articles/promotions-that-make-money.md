Promotions can fill a quiet Tuesday, win new customers and turn occasional diners into regulars. They can also train customers to only order when there's a discount, and leave you working flat out for no profit. The difference lies in how you design them.

## Start with a goal

Every promotion should have one clear purpose:

- **Win new customers**: introduce people to your food.
- **Move customers to direct ordering**: shift orders away from commission-based channels.
- **Fill quiet times**: boost Monday-to-Wednesday trade.
- **Increase order value**: encourage customers to add sides, drinks or desserts.
- **Reward loyalty**: thank regulars and keep them coming back.

If you can't say which goal a promotion serves, it probably isn't worth running.

## Do the maths first

Before launching any offer, work out:

1. **Your margin** on the items involved.
2. **The cost of the discount**: who uses it and how much it reduces your take.
3. **The extra orders needed** to make it worthwhile.

For example, a 20% discount on an order where your margin is 35% means you need significantly more orders, or bigger ones, just to stand still. If the promotion mostly goes to regulars who'd have ordered anyway, it costs you money.

## Promotions that usually work

### Direct-order discounts

A modest discount, such as 10% off orders through your own website or app, encourages customers to switch from marketplaces. If the marketplace charges commission, the discount can cost you less than the commission you save.

### Meal deals built on high-margin items

Bundles such as "curry, rice, naan and a drink" or a "family feast" raise average order value while offering customers clear value. Build them around dishes with strong margins, and check that the bundle price still makes money.

### Quiet-night offers

Offers limited to specific days, such as "Tuesday two-for-one on selected mains", bring in orders when the kitchen has spare capacity, without discounting your busiest nights.

### Free item over a threshold

"Free poppadoms and chutney on orders over £25" encourages customers to add a little more to their order. The cost to you is small compared with the extra spend.

### First-order welcome offers

A one-time discount for new customers ordering direct helps you build your own customer base, which you can then reach with future offers (with their consent).

### Loyalty rewards

"Every tenth order free" or points per pound spent rewards your best customers and gives them a reason not to drift to competitors.

## Promotions that often backfire

### Big blanket discounts

"30% off everything, every day" trains customers to expect cheap food and destroys margin. When the offer ends, orders often drop.

### Discounts on your busiest nights

If you're already full on Friday and Saturday, discounting those nights just gives money away.

### Too many offers at once

A menu covered in deals confuses customers and makes it hard to measure what works.

### Unclear terms

Hidden conditions frustrate customers and generate complaints, and can breach consumer protection rules.

## Stay within the rules

Promotions must follow consumer protection law and advertising rules:

- Offers must be **clear and honest**, with significant conditions stated upfront.
- **Reference prices** ("was £12, now £9") must be genuine.
- Advertising, including on social media, must follow the **CAP Code**, enforced by the Advertising Standards Authority.
- If you send offers by email or text, you generally need the customer's **consent** under PECR, apart from the limited "soft opt-in" for existing customers.

## Make promotions easy to use

- Use **simple codes**, such as "WELCOME10".
- Show offers clearly on your **website, app and menus**.
- Make sure staff **know about every live offer**.
- Set **start and end dates** and stick to them.

Online ordering systems let you set up discount codes, minimum order values and date limits automatically. [ChefOnline](https://www.chefonline.com/), for example, includes offer and loyalty tools in its ordering websites and apps.

## Measure every promotion

For each offer, track:

- **number of redemptions**
- **new customers** acquired
- **average order value** with and without the offer
- **margin** after the discount
- whether customers **came back** afterwards at full price.

Run a promotion for a fixed period, review it, and only repeat what genuinely works.

## Timing ideas through the year

- **January**: a quieter month, when value offers can help.
- **Ramadan**: iftar and suhoor meal packages, timed to sunset.
- **Big sporting events**: sharing platters for match nights.
- **Diwali and Eid**: celebration menus.
- **Christmas**: party platters and office lunch orders.
- **School holidays**: family meal deals.

## The bottom line

The best promotions have a clear goal, are built around high-margin items, target quiet times or direct ordering, and are measured carefully. Avoid blanket discounts and offers on your busiest nights, and keep every offer clear, honest and within the rules.
