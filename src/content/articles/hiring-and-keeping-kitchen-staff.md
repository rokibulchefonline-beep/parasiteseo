Finding and keeping skilled chefs and kitchen staff is one of the biggest challenges facing UK hospitality. Every time someone leaves, you pay for recruitment and training, and your food and service can suffer while you're short-staffed. Here's how to hire well and give people reasons to stay.

## Part 1: Hiring

### Write a clear, honest job advert

Good candidates want to know:

- the **role** and main duties
- **pay**, ideally a figure or range
- **hours**, including evenings and weekends
- **location** and transport links
- **what makes your kitchen a good place to work**: training, meals on shift, progression.

Vague adverts attract vague applications.

### Advertise in the right places

- **job boards** and hospitality-specific job sites
- **social media**, including local community groups
- **your own customers and network**: word of mouth is powerful
- **local colleges** with catering courses
- **staff referrals**, with a small bonus if a referred person stays.

### Interview and trial shifts

A practical **trial shift** shows you a candidate's skills and attitude, and shows them what the job is really like. If you ask someone to do a trial shift, be fair: the law on National Minimum Wage can apply to unpaid trials, so check GOV.UK guidance and pay where required.

### Do the legal checks

Before anyone starts work, you must:

- carry out a **right to work check** and keep a record
- give them a **written statement of employment particulars** on or before their first day
- pay at least the **National Minimum Wage or National Living Wage** for their age (check current rates on GOV.UK)
- assess them for **workplace pension auto-enrolment**.

Employers can face significant civil penalties for employing someone who doesn't have the right to work in the UK.

## Part 2: Onboarding

The first few weeks shape whether someone stays.

- **Prepare for their first day**: uniform, rota, introductions.
- **Train properly**: food safety, allergens, kitchen equipment, your recipes and your systems.
- **Pair them with an experienced colleague**.
- **Check in** at the end of the first week and the first month.

## Part 3: Keeping good people

### Pay fairly and on time

Pay doesn't have to be the highest in town, but it needs to be fair for the work and always correct and on time. Make sure tips are handled properly: under the **Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act**, employers must pass on all tips to workers fairly and have a written tipping policy.

### Predictable rotas

Last-minute changes are one of the biggest frustrations in hospitality. Publish rotas well in advance, share shifts fairly, and respect agreed days off. Rules giving workers the right to request more predictable working patterns are also being introduced, so keep an eye on GOV.UK guidance.

### Breaks and working time

Workers are entitled to rest breaks and limits on working time under the Working Time Regulations. A tired kitchen makes mistakes and burns people out.

### A safe and respectful kitchen

Kitchens can be high-pressure, but shouting, bullying and harassment drive good people away and can lead to legal claims. Employers also have a duty to take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their workers. Set clear standards and lead by example.

### Training and progression

People stay where they can grow. Offer:

- **skills training**: new cuisines, butchery, pastry, management
- **qualifications** such as food hygiene certificates
- **apprenticeships**, which may be partly funded
- a clear **path from kitchen porter to commis, chef de partie and beyond**.

### Recognition

Thank people, publicly and privately. Share good reviews that mention the food. Small gestures, like a staff meal, a bonus after a record weekend, or a night out, matter.

### Listen

Ask staff what would make the job better, and act on what you hear. Exit conversations with people who leave can reveal fixable problems.

## Part 4: Make the work easier

Good systems reduce stress for your team:

- **orders printed straight to the kitchen**, rather than shouted or re-typed
- **clear prep lists** and recipes
- **realistic order times** on busy nights
- **well-maintained equipment**.

Integrated online ordering and EPoS systems, such as those from [ChefOnline](https://www.chefonline.com/), help by sending orders directly to the kitchen and showing busy periods, so you can plan staffing.

## Part 5: Plan your staffing

Use your sales data to schedule the right number of people for each shift. Too few staff on a Friday leads to stress and mistakes; too many on a Tuesday wastes money. Review rotas against actual order volumes every few weeks.

## The bottom line

Write honest adverts, pay fairly, complete right to work and contract checks, onboard properly, publish predictable rotas, respect breaks and tips, offer training and progression, and keep the kitchen safe and respectful.
