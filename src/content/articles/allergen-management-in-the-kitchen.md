For customers with food allergies, a mistake can be life-threatening. For restaurants, getting allergens wrong can lead to serious harm, prosecution and lasting damage to your reputation. Good allergen management protects your customers and your business. Here's a practical approach.

## The legal basics

UK food businesses must:

- be able to tell customers if any of the **14 major allergens** are used as ingredients in a dish
- make that information **accurate, consistent and available**
- provide it for food sold **at a distance** (online or by phone) **before purchase** and **at delivery**.

The 14 allergens are: celery, cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, lupin, milk, molluscs, mustard, tree nuts, peanuts, sesame, soya, and sulphur dioxide/sulphites.

### Natasha's Law

Since October 2021, food that is **prepacked for direct sale (PPDS)** in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, meaning packed on the premises before a customer orders it, such as pre-made sandwiches or salads on display, must carry a **full ingredients label with allergens emphasised**. Scotland has equivalent rules. Most made-to-order takeaway food isn't PPDS, but if you pre-pack anything for display, these rules apply.

## Step 1: Build an allergen matrix

Create a chart listing every dish against the 14 allergens. For each dish, record:

- every ingredient, including sauces, marinades, oils, spice mixes and garnishes
- which allergens each ingredient contains, from supplier specifications and labels.

Keep this **allergen matrix** in the kitchen and at the counter, and update it whenever a recipe or supplier changes.

## Step 2: Check every supplier product

Ingredients that look simple can contain hidden allergens:

- **spice blends and curry pastes** can contain mustard, celery or nuts
- **ghee and butter** contain milk
- **some breads** contain sesame, egg or milk
- **stock cubes** can contain celery and gluten
- **Worcestershire sauce** contains fish.

Keep the product specifications or labels for every ingredient. If a supplier changes a product or you switch brands, recheck it.

## Step 3: Standardise recipes

If different chefs make the same dish in different ways, your allergen information becomes unreliable. Write down standard recipes and make sure everyone follows them. Substitutions should only happen with a manager's approval and an update to the matrix.

## Step 4: Control cross-contamination

Allergen safety isn't only about ingredients. Cross-contamination can happen through:

- **shared fryer oil**: for example, frying prawns and then chips in the same oil
- **shared utensils, pans and boards**
- **hands and cloths**
- **storage**: open containers of nuts or flour above other foods.

Practical controls include separate utensils (colour-coded where possible), dedicated preparation areas or times, cleaning between tasks, and covered, labelled storage. Be honest: if you can't guarantee a dish is free from an allergen, tell the customer.

## Step 5: Train every member of staff

Everyone who takes orders, cooks or packs food needs to know:

- what the 14 allergens are
- where the allergen matrix is
- how to answer customer questions accurately, and that it's fine to say "let me check"
- how to prepare and label an allergy-safe order
- what to do if a customer has a reaction.

The Food Standards Agency offers free online allergen training. Keep a record of who has completed it.

## Step 6: Take allergy orders carefully

### In person and by phone

Ask about allergies when taking orders, write them clearly on the ticket, and confirm with the kitchen.

### Online

Your online ordering system should:

- show allergen information, or clearly tell customers how to get it, **before they pay**
- allow customers to add **allergy notes**
- print those notes clearly on kitchen tickets.

Encourage customers with serious allergies to phone the restaurant as well, so a manager can confirm what's safe. Ordering platforms such as [ChefOnline](https://www.chefonline.com/) let restaurants manage allergen information on their online menus and pass order notes straight to the kitchen printer.

## Step 7: Label allergy orders at packing

For delivery and collection, allergen information must also be available **when the food is handed over**. Good practice includes:

- labelling allergy-safe dishes clearly, with the customer's name
- sealing containers
- packing allergy-safe orders separately where possible
- giving drivers clear instructions.

## Step 8: Plan for emergencies

Make sure staff know:

- the signs of a serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), such as swelling, difficulty breathing or collapse
- to **call 999** immediately and say "anaphylaxis"
- not to leave the person alone.

## Step 9: Review regularly

Review your allergen matrix and procedures:

- whenever you change a recipe, supplier or menu
- at least every few months, even if nothing has changed
- after any complaint or near miss.

## Why it's good for business

Customers with allergies, and the friends and families they eat with, are loyal to restaurants they trust. Clear allergen information and confident staff are a real competitive advantage. Strong allergen controls also support a good food hygiene rating.

## The bottom line

Build an allergen matrix, check every supplier product, standardise recipes, control cross-contamination, train every member of staff, handle online and phone orders carefully, label allergy orders at packing, and review regularly.
