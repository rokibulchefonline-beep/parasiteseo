For many UK takeaways, delivery is now a large share of the business. How you deliver affects your costs, your customer experience and your legal responsibilities. There are three broad options: your own drivers, a third-party courier service, or a marketplace's delivery network. Here's how to weigh them up.

## Option 1: Your own drivers

You employ (or engage) drivers who deliver only your orders.

### Advantages

- **Control**: you decide standards, routes and how drivers represent your brand.
- **Customer experience**: drivers who know your food and your regulars often give better service.
- **Cost per delivery** can be lower when you have steady volume.

### Disadvantages

- **Fixed costs**: you pay drivers even on quiet nights.
- **Management**: rotas, sickness cover, training and vehicle checks.
- **Legal responsibilities**: employment law, insurance and vehicle requirements.

### Legal points to check

- **Employment status**: whether drivers are employees, workers or genuinely self-employed affects rights such as the National Minimum Wage, holiday pay and pensions. The label in a contract isn't decisive; the reality of the working relationship is. GOV.UK has guidance on employment status, and ACAS offers free advice.
- **Right to work checks**: you must check every employee's right to work in the UK before they start.
- **Insurance**: delivering food for payment usually needs **hire and reward** or business delivery cover. Standard social, domestic and pleasure car insurance generally won't cover it. Make sure every vehicle used is properly insured, whether it belongs to you or the driver.
- **Vehicle safety**: vehicles must be roadworthy, taxed and, where applicable, have a valid MOT.

## Option 2: A third-party courier service

You use a local or national courier company that delivers for several businesses.

### Advantages

- **Flexible cost**: you typically pay per delivery, so quiet nights cost less.
- **Less admin**: the courier handles drivers, vehicles and insurance.
- **Scalability**: easier to cope with peaks.

### Disadvantages

- **Less control** over timing and driver behaviour.
- **Food may wait** if drivers are collecting from several restaurants.
- **Per-delivery fees** can add up at high volume.

### Questions to ask a courier

- What's the fee per delivery, and does it change by distance or time?
- What are typical collection and delivery times on Friday and Saturday nights?
- Who's responsible if food is late, damaged or missing?
- Are drivers trained in food handling and allergy awareness, such as keeping bags upright and sealed?
- Can deliveries be tracked?

## Option 3: Marketplace delivery

Some large marketplaces offer their own delivery networks.

### Advantages

- **Reach**: access to the marketplace's customer base.
- **No driver management** at all.

### Disadvantages

- **Higher commission** is often charged when the marketplace delivers.
- **Less control** over the customer experience, yet your restaurant still gets the review.
- **Customer data** usually stays with the marketplace.

## Comparing the costs

Work out your **cost per delivery** for each option. For your own drivers, include:

- wages (including holiday pay, employer National Insurance and pension where applicable)
- fuel or mileage allowances
- insurance
- vehicle costs, if you provide vehicles
- delivery bags and equipment.

Divide by the number of deliveries in a typical week. Compare that with courier fees and marketplace commission for the same volume. Many restaurants find that:

- at **low volumes**, couriers or marketplaces are cheaper
- at **high, steady volumes**, own drivers become more economical.

## A hybrid approach

Many takeaways mix models:

- **own drivers** for the core delivery area and peak nights
- **couriers** to cover overflow or the edges of the delivery area
- **collection** encouraged with discounts, which costs nothing to deliver.

## Getting delivery right, whatever the model

### Set a sensible delivery area

The further food travels, the worse it arrives. Draw your delivery area based on travel time, not just distance, and charge more for the outer zones if needed.

### Use time slots

Spreading orders across time slots reduces pressure on drivers and the kitchen.

### Pack for the journey

Use containers that seal well, keep hot and cold items separate, and put sauces in leak-proof pots. Good packaging protects your reputation.

### Communicate with customers

Accurate estimated times and order tracking reduce "where's my food?" calls. Ordering systems such as [ChefOnline](https://www.chefonline.com/) let you set delivery areas, minimum orders, delivery charges and time slots, and send orders straight to the kitchen.

### Deal with problems quickly

If an order arrives late or wrong, put it right fast. A quick refund or replacement often turns a complaint into loyalty.

## Food safety in transit

Hot food should be kept hot and delivered promptly. Clean insulated delivery bags regularly, keep raw and ready-to-eat foods apart, and make sure allergy-labelled items stay sealed. Your food safety management system should cover delivery.

## The bottom line

There's no single right answer. Own drivers give control and can be cheaper at volume, couriers offer flexibility, and marketplaces offer reach at a price. Work out your true cost per delivery, check employment status and insurance carefully, and consider a mix of models.
