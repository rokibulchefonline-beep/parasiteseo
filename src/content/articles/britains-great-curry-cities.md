Planning a food-focused weekend away? Few things capture modern Britain like its curry scenes. Each city has its own history, communities and signature dishes, shaped by the families who settled there and the tastes of local diners.

Here's a tour of the places that have shaped British Asian food, with tips for making the most of each.

## London: Brick Lane and beyond

### Brick Lane

**Brick Lane** in East London is probably the most famous curry street in the world. The area, known as **Banglatown**, became home to a large **Bangladeshi** community, many with roots in **Sylhet**, from the second half of the 20th century. Today the southern end of the street is lined with curry houses, alongside sari shops, grocers and sweet centres.

**Tips:**

- Walk the side streets as well as the main strip.
- Look for Bangladeshi specials such as **fish curries** and **bhortas**, not just the classics.
- Combine with a visit to **Old Spitalfields Market** and the Sunday markets.

### Beyond Brick Lane

London's South Asian food scene is vast:

- **Southall**, in west London, is known for Punjabi food and sweets.
- **Tooting**, in south London, is known for South Indian and Sri Lankan food.
- **Wembley** and **Harrow** have strong Gujarati and South Indian communities.
- **Whitechapel** has Pakistani grills and Bangladeshi cafés.
- **Central London** has some of the world's most acclaimed Indian fine-dining restaurants, several with Michelin stars.

## Birmingham: the Balti Triangle

Birmingham is credited with popularising the **balti**: a dish cooked quickly over a high flame and served in the same thin, flat-bottomed steel bowl it was cooked in. The name refers to the bowl, and the style is said to have Kashmiri and Pakistani roots.

The **Balti Triangle**, around Ladypool Road, Stoney Lane and Stratford Road in the Sparkbrook, Balsall Heath and Sparkhill areas, became the heart of Birmingham's balti scene from the 1970s and 1980s.

**Tips:**

- Order a balti with a **giant naan** to share, and eat it the traditional way, with bread rather than cutlery.
- Many restaurants are **bring your own** (BYO), but check before you go.

## Bradford

Bradford has long been celebrated for its curry houses, many founded by families from **Pakistan**, especially **Mirpur** in Azad Kashmir. It has been named Britain's **"Curry Capital"** several times in past national competitions.

Bradford is known for:

- **generous portions** and good value
- **karahi** dishes and **grilled meats**
- **family-run restaurants** with long histories
- late-night **curry cafés**.

**Tip:** pair your visit with the **National Science and Media Museum** and nearby **Saltaire**, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Manchester: the Curry Mile

**Wilmslow Road** in **Rusholme**, south of Manchester city centre, became known as the **Curry Mile**. Over the decades it has been lined with restaurants, takeaways, sweet centres, shisha lounges and late-night grills. The mix has changed over time, and now includes Middle Eastern and other cuisines alongside South Asian restaurants.

**Tips:**

- Visit in the evening, when the street comes alive with neon signs and crowds.
- Try the **sweet centres** for mithai (South Asian sweets).
- Combine with a visit to **Whitworth Park** and the **Whitworth** gallery nearby.

## Glasgow

Glasgow has a thriving curry scene and a strong claim to one of the most famous dishes in British food history. **Chicken tikka masala** is one of the dishes most often associated with the city, although its exact origin is disputed.

Glasgow's restaurants range from long-established curry houses to modern Indian and Pakistani kitchens, and the city has regularly done well in national curry awards.

**Tip:** Glasgow's West End and city centre have many of the best-known restaurants.

## Leicester: the Golden Mile

Leicester's **Belgrave Road**, known as the **Golden Mile** for its jewellery shops, is famous for **Gujarati** food: much of it **vegetarian**, with a wealth of snacks, sweets and thalis.

Leicester also hosts one of the **largest Diwali celebrations outside India**, drawing crowds for the lights switch-on and the festival itself.

**Tips:**

- Try **dhokla**, **kachori**, **pani puri** and a full **thali**.
- Visit around **Diwali** for the celebrations, but book accommodation early.

## Other places worth exploring

- **Cardiff** and **Newport**: long-established curry houses, some tracing their roots to seafarers.
- **Leeds**, **Sheffield** and **Newcastle**: strong curry scenes and regular award contenders.
- **Edinburgh**: a varied scene from classic curry houses to modern Indian.
- **Oldham**, **Blackburn** and **Rochdale**: excellent Pakistani and Bangladeshi restaurants.

## Tips for curry tourism

1. **Ask locals.** Taxi drivers, hotel staff and shopkeepers often know the best places.
2. **Look beyond the main strips.** The best food is sometimes a street or two away.
3. **Try regional specialities** rather than the standard menu.
4. **Check hygiene ratings** at [ratings.food.gov.uk](https://ratings.food.gov.uk/).
5. **Book ahead** at weekends.
6. **Check BYO policies** before you go.

## Find award-winning restaurants

Wherever you travel, shortlists and winners from the [Asian Restaurant & Takeaway Awards (ARTA)](https://www.artauk.com/) are a useful guide. ARTA recognises restaurants and takeaways across the UK's regions based on public votes, online reviews and food hygiene ratings.

## The bottom line

Britain's curry cities tell the story of the communities who built them. From Brick Lane's Bangladeshi kitchens to Birmingham's baltis, Bradford's karahis and Leicester's Gujarati thalis, a curry tour is one of the tastiest ways to explore the UK.
