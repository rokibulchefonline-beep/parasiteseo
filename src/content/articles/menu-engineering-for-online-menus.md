Your menu is your most important sales tool. Online, it does the job of a waiter: suggesting, describing and guiding choices. **Menu engineering** means designing your menu so customers easily find what they want and naturally choose dishes that are good for your business.

Small changes can make a real difference to your average order value and your profit.

## Step 1: Know your numbers

Menu engineering starts with two figures for every dish:

- **Popularity**: how many you sell, from your EPoS or ordering reports.
- **Profitability**: the gross profit per dish, meaning selling price minus ingredient cost.

To calculate ingredient cost, cost out each recipe: the protein, sauce base, oil, spices, garnish and packaging. It takes time, but it's the foundation of everything else.

## Step 2: Sort dishes into four groups

Plot each dish on two axes, popularity and profitability, and you'll get four groups.

### Stars: popular and profitable

These are your best dishes. **Promote them.** Put them at the top of categories, feature them in "popular" sections and use your best photos for them.

### Plough horses: popular but lower margin

Customers love them, but they don't make you much money. Options include:

- **reviewing portion sizes**, carefully
- **adjusting recipes** to reduce cost without affecting quality
- **modest price increases**
- **pairing them** with high-margin sides.

### Puzzles: profitable but unpopular

These make good money but don't sell. Try:

- **better descriptions** and **photos**
- **moving them** to more visible positions
- **renaming** them
- **featuring them** as specials.

### Dogs: neither popular nor profitable

Consider removing them. Every dish adds complexity to your kitchen, stock and menu. Cutting weak dishes makes your menu easier to read and your kitchen faster.

## Step 3: Use the most visible positions

Online, attention isn't spread evenly. The most-viewed positions tend to be:

- the **top of the menu**, often a "popular" or "recommended" section
- the **first few items in each category**
- items **with photos**.

Put your stars and your most profitable puzzles there.

## Step 4: Write descriptions that sell

A good description makes customers hungry and helps them choose with confidence.

- **Be specific**: "Chicken curry" tells a customer little. "Tender chicken thigh slow-cooked in a rich tomato, ginger and fenugreek sauce" makes them want it.
- **Mention heat level and key ingredients.**
- **Highlight what's special**: family recipe, chef's speciality, regional dish.
- **Keep it short**: one or two sentences.

## Step 5: Use photos wisely

Good photos of real dishes increase confidence and orders. Use natural light, keep backgrounds clean and make sure the photo matches what customers receive. A poor photo can do more harm than no photo.

## Step 6: Smart add-ons and upselling

Online ordering systems can prompt customers to add extras. Use this thoughtfully:

- **Rice and bread** prompts when someone adds a curry.
- **Sides and starters** as suggestions at checkout.
- **Drinks and desserts**, which often have strong margins.
- **"Make it a meal"** options.

Keep prompts relevant and limited. Too many pop-ups annoy customers.

## Step 7: Meal deals and bundles

Bundles can raise average order value while offering customers good value:

- **meal for one**: main, rice, bread and a drink
- **meal for two**: starters, two mains, rice, bread and a side
- **family feast**: a larger mix for four or more.

Build bundles around your stars and high-margin items, and check the maths so the bundle is still profitable after any discount.

## Step 8: Keep the menu manageable

Long menus are common in British curry houses, but they come with costs:

- customers take longer to decide, and some give up
- the kitchen needs more stock and more prep
- consistency suffers
- food waste rises.

Many successful restaurants trim their menus and focus on doing fewer dishes brilliantly. Consider a core menu plus a small rotating specials section.

## Step 9: Price with care

- Avoid **pound signs everywhere**, where your platform allows. Some research suggests currency symbols make people more price-conscious.
- **Don't list dishes strictly by price**, as it encourages customers to hunt for the cheapest.
- Review prices regularly as ingredient costs change.

## Step 10: Test, measure, repeat

Make **one change at a time**, and track results for a few weeks:

- average order value
- sales of the dish you changed
- overall profit.

If a change works, keep it. If not, try something else.

## Online vs in-house menus

Your online menu doesn't have to be identical to your dine-in menu. Some dishes travel better than others. Consider removing dishes that suffer in transit, or adding travel-friendly options only available for takeaway.
