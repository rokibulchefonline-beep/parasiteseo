Property licensing is one of the areas where London landlords most often get caught out. The rules vary from borough to borough, and sometimes from ward to ward within the same borough. Schemes start, end and change regularly. And the penalties for letting a property without a required licence can be severe.

This guide explains the three main types of licensing in England and how to check what applies to your property.

## Why licensing matters

Letting a property that requires a licence without one is a **criminal offence**. Consequences can include:

- **unlimited fines** on prosecution, or substantial **civil penalties** from the council
- **rent repayment orders**, which can require you to repay rent to tenants or the council
- restrictions on your ability to regain possession of the property
- damage to your reputation and your ability to hold future licences.

Councils actively enforce licensing, and tenants can apply for rent repayment orders themselves.

## 1. Mandatory HMO licensing

A **house in multiple occupation (HMO)** is, broadly, a property let to **three or more people** who form **more than one household** and share facilities such as a kitchen, bathroom or toilet.

A **mandatory HMO licence** is required across England if the property is occupied by:

- **five or more people**
- forming **more than one household**
- sharing basic amenities.

This applies regardless of the number of storeys. Some purpose-built flats are treated differently, so check the detailed rules.

A "household" can be a single person, a couple, or members of the same family living together. So five unrelated sharers need a licence, while a family of five doesn't.

## 2. Additional licensing

Councils can introduce **additional licensing** schemes to extend licensing to **smaller HMOs** that aren't covered by the mandatory scheme, for example properties let to **three or four sharers**.

Additional licensing can cover the **whole borough** or **specific areas**. Many London boroughs operate additional licensing.

## 3. Selective licensing

**Selective licensing** goes further: it requires a licence for **ordinary privately rented homes**, including those let to a single household such as a family or couple, within a designated area.

Councils introduce selective licensing where they have concerns about issues such as poor property conditions, anti-social behaviour or deprivation. Schemes often cover **specific wards** rather than a whole borough, although some cover much larger areas.

## How to check what applies

Because schemes vary so much, always check the details for your exact property:

1. **Visit your council's website.** Search for "private rented sector licensing" or "property licensing".
2. **Check the designated area.** Councils publish maps or postcode checkers showing which areas are covered by additional or selective schemes.
3. **Check the dates.** Schemes usually last up to five years. Check when the current scheme started and ends, and whether a renewal or new scheme is being consulted on.
4. **Consider the occupancy.** Count the number of occupiers and households, and check again whenever occupancy changes.
5. **If in doubt, ask.** Contact the council's licensing team or a professional agent.

## Applying for a licence

Applications are usually made online and require:

- details of the property, landlord and manager
- the number of occupiers, households, rooms and facilities
- safety certificates: gas, electrical, EPC, fire alarm and emergency lighting where relevant
- floor plans, for HMOs
- the application fee.

The council must be satisfied that the licence holder is a **"fit and proper person"** and that management arrangements are satisfactory.

## Licence conditions

Licences come with conditions that you must comply with throughout the licence period. These typically cover:

- **maximum numbers of occupiers**
- **minimum room sizes**: for HMOs, national minimum bedroom sizes apply
- **fire safety**: alarms, fire doors and escape routes
- **gas and electrical safety**
- **waste storage and disposal**
- **management standards**: repairs, inspections and dealing with anti-social behaviour
- **providing information** to tenants.

Keep evidence that you comply: certificates, inspection records and correspondence.

## HMO management regulations

Even where no licence is required, **all HMOs** are subject to **management regulations** that set duties on managers, covering things such as fire safety, water supply, common parts, living accommodation and waste.

## Planning permission

Licensing and planning are separate. In some areas, councils have removed permitted development rights (through an **Article 4 direction**), meaning planning permission is needed to convert a family home into a small HMO. Check planning rules before converting.

## The changing landscape

Private renting in England is changing significantly under the **Renters' Rights Act**, which became law in 2025. It introduces reforms to tenancies and property standards alongside existing licensing rules. Landlords should keep up to date with official guidance on **GOV.UK** and their council's website.
