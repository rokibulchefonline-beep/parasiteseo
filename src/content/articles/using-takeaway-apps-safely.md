Food ordering apps have made takeaways easier than ever. They also hold a surprising amount of personal information: your name, home address, phone number, order history and often your saved payment details. That makes them worth protecting like any other important account.

The good news is that a few simple habits go a long way.

## 1. Download apps only from official stores

Only install food ordering apps from Apple's **App Store** or **Google Play**. Before installing, check:

- the **developer name** matches the business you expect
- the app has a reasonable number of reviews and downloads
- the permissions it asks for make sense (a food app needs your location for delivery, but not your contacts or microphone).

Fake apps and look-alike websites do exist, particularly around big promotions. Genuine platforms, such as the [ChefOnline](https://www.chefonline.co.uk/) app for iOS and Android, will be listed under their official developer name.

## 2. Use a strong, unique password

Password reuse is one of the biggest risks online. If you use the same password for your food app as for a website that has been hacked, criminals can simply try it on your account. This is known as "credential stuffing", and it's very common.

The National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) recommends:

- using a **different password for every important account**
- creating passwords from **three random words**, such as "teapot-lighthouse-marmalade"
- using a **password manager**, built into most phones and browsers, to remember them for you.

## 3. Turn on extra security where it's offered

If an app offers **two-step verification** (sometimes called two-factor authentication), switch it on. It means that even if someone learns your password, they can't log in without a code sent to your phone or generated by an app.

Also consider using your phone's **biometric lock** (Face ID or fingerprint) for apps that store payment details.

## 4. Pay safely

- **Credit cards** offer extra protection under Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act for purchases between £100 and £30,000. Most takeaways are below that, but credit and debit cards both have chargeback schemes that can help if something goes wrong.
- **Apple Pay and Google Pay** don't share your actual card number with the merchant.
- **Never pay by bank transfer** to someone claiming to be a restaurant or delivery company through social media or text messages. Legitimate ordering apps don't work that way.

## 5. Check before you enter card details

On websites, check that:

- the web address is correct, with no misspellings or extra words
- the address starts with **https**
- you reached the page from a link you trust, not from an unexpected text or social media advert.

## 6. Be wary of "too good to be true" offers

Scammers know people love a food deal. Be cautious of:

- messages offering huge discounts or free food for a limited time
- social media posts asking you to "claim a voucher" by entering your card details
- links that don't go to the real app or website.

If an offer is genuine, you'll find it inside the official app or on the restaurant's own website.

## 7. Watch out for fake delivery messages

Criminals often pretend to be delivery companies. Common tricks include:

- texts saying your order "couldn't be delivered" and asking you to pay a small fee
- calls asking you to "confirm" your card details
- emails with links to fake tracking pages.

A genuine food app won't ask you to confirm your full card details by text or phone. In the UK you can:

- forward suspicious texts to **7726** (free on most networks)
- forward suspicious emails to **report@phishing.gov.uk**.

## 8. Review your saved details

Every few months, open your food apps and check:

- your **saved addresses**, and remove old ones
- your **saved cards**, and remove any you don't use
- your **order history**, and look for anything you don't recognise.

If you see unfamiliar orders, change your password immediately and contact both the app's support team and your bank.

## 9. Keep your phone updated

Software updates fix security weaknesses. Turn on automatic updates for your phone's operating system and your apps.

## 10. Think about shared devices

If you share a tablet or laptop with others, log out of apps with saved cards, or make sure payment needs a PIN or fingerprint.

## Your data rights

Under UK GDPR, you have the right to know what personal data a company holds about you and, in many cases, to ask for it to be corrected or deleted. The app's privacy notice should explain how to make a request. You can also choose whether to receive marketing messages, and unsubscribe at any time.

If you're unhappy with how a company handles your data, you can complain to the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO).

## What to do if your account is compromised

1. Change your password straight away, and anywhere else you've used it.
2. Contact the app's customer support.
3. Call your bank if a saved card may have been used.
4. Report fraud to **Action Fraud** (England, Wales and Northern Ireland) or **Police Scotland** by calling 101.

## The bottom line

Food ordering apps are safe to use when you treat them like any other important account. Download from official stores, use strong unique passwords, pay with protected methods and be sceptical of unexpected messages.
