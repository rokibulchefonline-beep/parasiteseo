Ingredients, energy, rent, packaging and wages have all risen for UK restaurants and takeaways in recent years. Many owners delay raising prices for fear of losing customers, and end up working harder for less. Pricing doesn't have to be guesswork. Here's a practical approach.

## Step 1: Know your food cost for every dish

Start with what each dish actually costs you to make:

1. List every ingredient in the recipe, including oil, spices, garnish and the rice or bread served with it.
2. Work out the cost of the quantity used, from your latest supplier invoices.
3. Add **packaging** for takeaway and delivery: containers, lids, bags, napkins and cutlery.

This gives you the **plate cost** (or "food cost") of each dish. It takes time the first time, but once it's in a spreadsheet, updating it when supplier prices change is quick.

## Step 2: Understand your target food cost percentage

Your **food cost percentage** is the plate cost divided by the selling price (excluding VAT). Many restaurants aim for food costs somewhere around a quarter to a third of the selling price, but the right figure depends on your other costs. A takeaway with low rent and few staff can work with a different percentage from a city-centre restaurant.

For example, if a curry costs £3.20 to make and you sell it for £11.50 including VAT:

- selling price excluding VAT (at 20%) = £9.58
- food cost percentage = £3.20 ÷ £9.58 = 33%.

If that's above your target, you have three options: raise the price, reduce the cost, or accept a lower margin on that dish because it brings in other sales.

## Step 3: Remember VAT

Most hot takeaway food is **standard-rated for VAT** in the UK if your business is VAT registered, and that VAT comes out of what the customer pays. When calculating margins, always work from prices **excluding VAT**. HMRC's guidance on catering and takeaway food explains which items are standard-rated and which are zero-rated (some cold takeaway food, for example). If you're unsure, ask your accountant.

## Step 4: Cover your overheads

Food is only part of the picture. Your prices also need to cover:

- **wages**, including employer National Insurance and pension contributions
- **rent and business rates**
- **energy**
- **card fees** and **ordering platform fees** or commission
- **delivery costs**: drivers, fuel and insurance
- **marketing**
- **repairs and equipment**.

Add up your monthly overheads and divide by your typical number of orders per month. That tells you roughly how much each order needs to contribute before you make any profit.

## Step 5: Check your channel costs

The same dish can be much less profitable on one channel than another. A meal sold through a marketplace charging commission costs you more than the same meal ordered through your own website or over the counter.

Some restaurants set **slightly higher prices on commission-based marketplaces** to cover the fee, while keeping their best prices for direct orders. If you do this, be transparent and consistent. Commission-free ordering, such as through [ChefOnline](https://www.chefonline.com/), means the price the customer pays isn't eroded by a percentage fee on each order.

## Step 6: Raise prices the smart way

When you need to increase prices, how you do it matters.

### Small, regular increases

Several small rises are generally less noticeable than one large jump. Review prices at least twice a year.

### Not every dish needs to rise equally

Raise prices most on dishes where your margin has fallen furthest, or on popular signature dishes customers love. Keep a few well-known items competitively priced as "anchors".

### Avoid psychological jumps

Moving from £9.95 to £10.45 crosses a threshold customers notice. Sometimes it's better to adjust portion composition or add value instead.

### Add value, not just cost

Bundle dishes into **meal deals** that offer good value while using your highest-margin items, or include a free side with direct orders.

## Step 7: Reduce costs without cutting quality

Before raising prices, look for savings that customers won't notice:

- **review suppliers** and compare prices at least once a year
- **buy in bulk** for long-life staples, if you have storage
- **cut waste**: measure it, and order to real demand
- **standardise portions** so costs are predictable
- **review packaging**: right-sized containers cost less and travel better.

Never cut corners on food safety or allergen controls to save money.

## Step 8: Watch the competition, but don't chase it

Know what nearby restaurants charge, but don't automatically match them. If your food, service and reputation are better, you can charge more. Racing to be the cheapest usually ends with nobody making money.

## Step 9: Communicate honestly

If you raise prices significantly, a short, honest note can help: "Like every independent restaurant, our costs have gone up. We've kept increases as small as we can, and we haven't cut portions or quality." Regulars usually understand.

## Step 10: Update prices everywhere at once

Make sure prices match on your website, app, printed menus, EPoS and any marketplaces. Mismatched prices frustrate customers and staff. A system where you change the menu once and it updates across your website, app and till saves time and avoids mistakes.

## A simple pricing checklist

- Do I know the plate cost of every dish?
- Am I calculating margins excluding VAT?
- Do my prices cover overheads and channel fees?
- When did I last review supplier prices?
- Are my prices consistent across every channel?

## The bottom line

Pricing should be based on numbers, not nerves. Cost every dish, work from prices excluding VAT, cover your overheads and channel fees, raise prices in small regular steps, and look for savings that don't affect quality.
