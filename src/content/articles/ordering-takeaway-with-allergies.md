For anyone with a food allergy, or a parent ordering for an allergic child, a takeaway can be a stressful experience. You're trusting a kitchen you can't see to get it right. The good news is that UK law gives you a clear right to allergen information, and a few simple steps make ordering much safer.

## The 14 major allergens

UK food law identifies 14 major allergens that businesses must tell you about if they're used as ingredients:

1. celery
2. cereals containing gluten (such as wheat, rye, barley and oats)
3. crustaceans (such as prawns, crabs and lobster)
4. eggs
5. fish
6. lupin
7. milk
8. molluscs (such as mussels and oysters)
9. mustard
10. tree nuts (such as almonds, cashews and walnuts)
11. peanuts
12. sesame
13. soya
14. sulphur dioxide and sulphites (above a certain level).

Food businesses must be able to tell you if any dish contains any of these, including in sauces, dressings, oils, marinades and garnishes.

## Your rights when ordering online or by phone

When food is sold "at a distance", such as through a website, app or phone order, the Food Standards Agency says allergen information must be available at two points:

- **Before you complete your purchase**, for example in the online menu or by speaking to staff on the phone.
- **When the food is delivered**, for example through labels or stickers on the containers, or information provided with the order.

In other words, you shouldn't have to guess, and you shouldn't be told "it should be fine" without the information to back it up.

## Why Asian takeaways need extra care

South Asian cooking is wonderfully varied, but some allergens are common:

- **Nuts and peanuts**: korma and pasanda sauces often use ground almonds or cashews, and peshwari naan usually contains nuts. Some restaurants use nut-based pastes in other dishes too.
- **Milk**: cream, butter, ghee, yoghurt and paneer appear in many dishes.
- **Mustard**: mustard seeds and mustard oil are common in Bengali and South Indian cooking.
- **Gluten**: naan, chapati, samosa pastry, bhaji batter (which may include wheat flour) and some sauces.
- **Sesame**: found in some breads and chutneys.
- **Crustaceans and fish**: prawn and fish dishes are often cooked in the same kitchen as everything else.

This doesn't mean people with allergies can't enjoy a curry. It means asking specific questions is essential.

## Questions to ask before you order

- Does this dish contain my allergen, **including in the sauce, marinade, oil or garnish**?
- Is the same oil used for frying foods that contain my allergen?
- Are the same pans, utensils or tandoor used?
- Can the kitchen make a safe version, and how will it be kept separate and labelled?
- Who can I speak to if I have a question when the food arrives?

If staff aren't sure, or can't give clear answers, it's safest not to order.

## Use order notes, then call

Most online ordering platforms, including [ChefOnline](https://www.chefonline.co.uk/), let you add notes to your order. Notes are useful for recording your allergy, but they can be missed during a busy service. For a serious allergy, it's safest to:

1. Check the allergen information on the online menu.
2. Add a clear note to the order.
3. **Phone the restaurant** to confirm the kitchen has seen it and can cater for you safely.

## When the food arrives

- Check that dishes are labelled as expected.
- If anything looks different from what you ordered, don't eat it until you've checked with the restaurant.
- Keep any medication, such as an adrenaline auto-injector, close by, as you would anywhere else.

## Precautionary labels

You may see phrases such as "may contain nuts" on menus. These are used where there's a risk of cross-contamination that the business can't rule out. Take them seriously, particularly for severe allergies.

## Choosing restaurants you can trust

- Build a relationship with one or two restaurants that understand your needs.
- Check food hygiene ratings, which reflect how well a kitchen manages food safety generally.
- Read reviews from other customers with allergies.

## If something goes wrong

If you or someone you're with has a severe allergic reaction, **call 999** immediately, use an adrenaline auto-injector if one has been prescribed, and say "anaphylaxis".

If you believe a business gave you incorrect allergen information, you can report it to the local council's food safety team.

## The bottom line

You have a legal right to know what's in your food. Check the menu, ask specific questions, add a note and phone ahead for serious allergies. Most restaurants want to get it right, and clear communication gives them the best chance to do so.

*This article is general information and not medical advice. Speak to your GP or allergy specialist about managing your allergy.*
