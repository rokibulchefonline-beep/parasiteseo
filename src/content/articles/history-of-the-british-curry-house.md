Curry is so woven into British life that it's easy to forget how it got here. The story of the British curry house spans more than two centuries, and it's a story of trade and empire, migration and enterprise, and a cuisine that adapted, evolved and became part of the national identity.

## Early encounters

British contact with South Asian food goes back to the **East India Company**, founded in 1600. Company employees who returned from India brought a taste for spiced dishes, and recipes for "currey" appeared in English cookbooks in the 18th century. Hannah Glasse's famous *The Art of Cookery*, first published in 1747, included a recipe for making "a currey the India way".

By the late 18th century, some London coffee houses served curry, and curry powder was being sold commercially.

## The first curry house: 1810

In 1810, **Sake Dean Mahomed**, a traveller and entrepreneur from Patna, in what was then the Bengal Presidency, opened the **Hindoostanee Coffee House** on George Street, near Portman Square in London. It's widely regarded as Britain's first Indian restaurant.

Dean Mahomed had served in the East India Company's army, moved to Ireland and then to England, and was also the first Indian author to publish a book in English. His coffee house offered "Indian dishes in the highest perfection" and hookahs, and aimed to attract returning Company officials.

The business struggled and closed within a few years. But curry had arrived on the British restaurant scene. Dean Mahomed later found fame in Brighton as a "shampooing surgeon", offering therapeutic Indian massage and baths.

## The oldest survivor

**Veeraswamy**, on Regent Street, opened in **1926**. It was founded by Edward Palmer, whose family had connections to India, and it's often described as the **oldest surviving Indian restaurant in the UK**. Over the decades it has served royalty, politicians and celebrities.

## The seafarers

In the early 20th century, many **lascars**, sailors from South Asia working on British merchant ships, settled in port cities such as London, Liverpool and Cardiff. Many came from **Sylhet**, in what is now Bangladesh. Some opened cafés and boarding houses serving food to fellow seafarers, laying the foundations for later restaurants.

## The post-war boom

The biggest growth came after the Second World War. From the 1950s and 1960s onwards, many people from **Pakistan** (including what became **Bangladesh** in 1971) and **India** came to Britain, often to fill labour shortages in factories, mills and transport.

Many took over **fish and chip shops** and cafés, adding curry and rice to the menu alongside British staples. Over time, curry became the main attraction.

**Bangladeshi**, and especially **Sylheti**, families played a central role. It's often said that a large majority of the UK's "Indian" restaurants were in fact founded and run by Bangladeshi families.

## A cuisine of its own

The British curry house menu evolved to suit British tastes, ingredients and restaurant kitchens:

- a **standard base sauce** allowed kitchens to produce a wide menu quickly
- dishes were categorised by **heat level**, from korma to vindaloo and phall
- **tandoori** cooking became popular from the 1960s and 1970s
- **pints of lager**, **poppadoms** and **chutney trays** became part of the ritual.

Much of this menu would be unfamiliar in South Asia. It's a genuinely British Asian creation.

### Chicken tikka masala

**Chicken tikka masala** became perhaps the most famous dish of all. Its origin is disputed, and several cities and restaurants claim it. **Glasgow**'s claim is one of the best known. In 2001, Foreign Secretary Robin Cook famously described chicken tikka masala as "a true British national dish", reflecting how Britain absorbs and adapts influences.

## Regional flavours

As the curry house spread across the country, regional scenes developed:

- **Birmingham** popularised the **balti**, cooked quickly and served in a thin steel bowl, centred on the "Balti Triangle".
- **Bradford** became famous for its Pakistani curry houses and was repeatedly named Britain's "Curry Capital".
- **Manchester's Curry Mile**, on Wilmslow Road in Rusholme, became a landmark.
- **Brick Lane** in East London became known as **Banglatown**.
- **Leicester**'s Belgrave Road became known for Gujarati vegetarian food.

## Challenges and change

In recent years, the traditional curry house has faced real challenges:

- **staff shortages**, particularly of skilled chefs
- **rising costs** of ingredients, energy and rent
- **changing habits**, including the rise of takeaways and delivery apps
- **generational change**, as some children of founding families choose other careers.

Many curry houses have closed. But the story isn't one of decline alone.

## A new chapter

New generations are reinventing British Asian food:

- **regional** restaurants focusing on the food of specific places, such as Kerala, Punjab, Lahore or Sylhet
- **street food** and **small plates**
- **fine dining**, with Indian restaurants winning Michelin stars
- **modern takeaways**, with online ordering and delivery
- traditional curry houses **refreshing** their menus and interiors.

## Celebrating the legacy

Initiatives such as the [Asian Restaurant & Takeaway Awards (ARTA)](https://www.artauk.com/) celebrate the restaurants, takeaways, chefs and families keeping the tradition alive, and recognise its place in British culture.

## The bottom line

From Sake Dean Mahomed's coffee house in 1810 to today's award-winning restaurants, the British curry house is a story of migration, enterprise and adaptation. It's a cuisine that belongs as much to Britain as to South Asia.
