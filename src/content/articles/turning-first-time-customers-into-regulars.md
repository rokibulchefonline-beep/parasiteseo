Every takeaway owner knows the value of regulars: the family who orders every Friday, the office that orders every payday, the couple who always ask for "the usual". Regulars provide steady income, recommend you to friends and forgive the occasional mistake.

Winning a new customer often costs far more than keeping an existing one, especially if that new customer came through an app charging commission. So turning first-time customers into regulars is one of the most profitable things you can do.

## 1. Get the first order right

Nothing builds loyalty like a great first experience. That means:

- **the right order**: every dish, side and sauce, exactly as requested
- **hot food**, packed to travel well
- **on time**, or at least as promised
- **the little extras**: napkins, cutlery, chutneys and anything the customer asked for.

Before any bag leaves, check it against the order. A missing naan can undo an otherwise perfect meal.

## 2. Add a personal touch

Small gestures cost little but stay with people:

- a handwritten "Thank you, enjoy your meal!" on the bag
- a free poppadom or small dessert for first-time customers
- a note recognising a special occasion mentioned in the order.

These touches remind customers that there are real people behind the food.

## 3. Make it easy to order again, direct

If a new customer found you on a marketplace, give them a reason to order direct next time:

- a **leaflet or sticker** in the bag with your website address, app links and a QR code
- a **direct-order discount**, such as 10% off their next order through your own website
- **collection offers** for customers who live nearby.

Every customer who switches to direct ordering saves you commission on all their future orders.

## 4. Build a loyalty scheme

Loyalty schemes give regulars a reason to keep choosing you:

- **stamp cards**: simple and effective ("every tenth meal free")
- **points-based schemes**: points per pound spent, redeemable against future orders
- **tiered rewards**: extra perks for your most frequent customers
- **birthday offers**: a free dessert or discount in their birthday month.

Online ordering systems and apps can manage points automatically, which makes schemes easier to run and more appealing to customers.

## 5. Stay in touch, within the rules

Email and text marketing can bring customers back, but in the UK they're governed by **UK GDPR** and the **Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR)**.

### Consent

In general, you need a person's **consent** before sending them marketing emails or texts. Consent must be freely given, specific and clearly shown. A pre-ticked box doesn't count.

### The "soft opt-in"

There's a limited exception for existing customers, known as the **soft opt-in**. You can send marketing emails or texts to existing customers if:

- you collected their contact details **during a sale** (or negotiations for a sale)
- you're marketing **your own similar products or services**
- you gave them a **clear, simple opportunity to opt out** when you collected their details
- you give them a chance to opt out **in every message**.

### Good practice

- Keep **records** of how and when people opted in.
- Make **unsubscribing easy**, and act on it promptly.
- Don't send too often.
- Explain your use of data in your **privacy notice**.

The **Information Commissioner's Office (ICO)** publishes detailed guidance on direct marketing.

## 6. Send messages people want

When you do contact customers, make it worthwhile:

- a genuine offer on a quiet night
- a new dish or seasonal special
- holiday opening hours
- award news or community events.

Avoid bombarding people. One or two relevant messages a month is plenty for most takeaways.

## 7. Ask for feedback, and act on it

Ask customers how their meal was, through a short survey, a follow-up email or a card in the bag. When someone tells you something went wrong:

- **thank them** for letting you know
- **apologise** sincerely
- **put it right**, with a replacement, refund or voucher
- **fix the cause**, so it doesn't happen again.

Customers whose complaints are handled well often become more loyal than customers who never had a problem.

## 8. Remember your regulars

With a good ordering system, you can see customers' order history. Use it:

- greet regulars by name when they collect
- recognise their usual order
- surprise frequent customers with an occasional thank-you.

## 9. Stay consistent

Loyalty depends on consistency. Regulars come back because they know exactly what they'll get. Standardise recipes, portion sizes and packaging, so the food is just as good on a busy Saturday as on a quiet Tuesday.

## 10. Measure loyalty

Track:

- the **percentage of orders from repeat customers**
- how often regulars order
- how many first-time customers return within 30 or 60 days
- the proportion of orders through direct channels.

If these numbers are rising, your loyalty efforts are working.
