Curry is so woven into British life that it's easy to forget how it got here. The story spans more than two centuries.

## The first curry house

In 1810, **Sake Dean Mahomed**, a traveller and entrepreneur from Bengal, opened the **Hindoostanee Coffee House** in London, widely regarded as Britain's first Indian restaurant. It closed within a few years, but curry had arrived.

## The oldest survivor

**Veeraswamy**, on Regent Street, opened in 1926 and is often described as the oldest surviving Indian restaurant in the UK.

## The post-war boom

From the 1950s and 1960s onwards, many people from what is now Bangladesh, especially the Sylhet region, as well as from Pakistan and India, came to Britain and opened restaurants. Many of the curry houses on Britain's high streets were founded by Bangladeshi families.

## A cuisine of its own

The British curry house menu, with its kormas, madras, vindaloos and tikka masalas, evolved to suit British tastes. Chicken tikka masala has been described as a national dish, and several cities claim its invention, with Glasgow among the best-known claimants.

## Regional flavours

- **Birmingham** popularised the balti.
- **Bradford** and **Manchester's Curry Mile** in Rusholme became famous for their curry scenes.
- **Brick Lane** in East London became known as Banglatown.

## Today

Curry houses face rising costs and staffing challenges, but new generations are reinventing the cuisine with regional dishes, street food and fine dining. Initiatives such as the [Asian Restaurant & Takeaway Awards](https://www.artauk.com/) celebrate the people keeping the tradition alive.
