Selling a flat in East London can move quickly in the right market, but flats, and especially leasehold flats, come with extra paperwork that can slow things down if you're not prepared. Knowing what to expect at each stage helps you avoid delays and fall-throughs.

Here's the process in England, step by step.

## Step 1: Understand your property and your goals

Before calling any agents, think about:

- **why you're selling** and your ideal timescale
- **where you're moving to**, and whether you need to buy at the same time (a chain) or can move into rented accommodation
- **your mortgage**: check for early repayment charges and whether your mortgage is portable.

## Step 2: Get valuations

Invite **two or three local agents** to value your flat. A good valuation is based on:

- recent sales of similar flats nearby
- your flat's size, layout, floor level and condition
- outside space, views and natural light
- the building: its condition, facilities and management
- **lease length**, service charges and ground rent
- current market conditions and buyer demand.

Ask each agent how they reached their figure and which comparable sales they used. Be cautious of any agent who quotes far above everyone else. An inflated asking price can leave your flat sitting on the market, which can end up reducing the price you achieve.

## Step 3: Choose your agent

Compare agents on:

- **local knowledge**: how many similar flats have they sold nearby recently?
- **fees**: percentage or fixed fee, and whether VAT is included
- **contract terms**: length, notice period, and whether it's sole agency or multi-agency
- **marketing**: photography, floor plans, online portals and their buyer database
- **communication**: who will be your point of contact?

Every estate agent in the UK must belong to a government-approved **redress scheme**, such as The Property Ombudsman or the Property Redress Scheme. Ask which one.

## Step 4: Prepare your paperwork early

This is where many flat sales get delayed. Start gathering documents as soon as you decide to sell.

### Essentials

- **Energy Performance Certificate (EPC)**: you need a valid EPC before marketing.
- **Proof of identity**: agents and solicitors must carry out anti-money-laundering checks.
- **Title deeds**, usually held electronically by HM Land Registry.

### Leasehold documents

- **your lease**
- details of **service charges** and **ground rent**, with recent statements
- contact details for the **managing agent** and **freeholder**
- any planned **major works** or **section 20 notices**
- **building insurance** details.

The buyer's solicitor will request a **management pack** (often called an LPE1) from the managing agent. This can take several weeks and there's usually a fee, so it's worth requesting it early.

### Building safety

For flats in some buildings, particularly taller buildings, buyers and lenders may ask for building safety information, such as an **EWS1 form** or details of any remediation works. Speak to your managing agent early if this may apply.

### Other documents

- guarantees and warranties, such as for windows or electrical work
- building regulations certificates for any alterations
- gas safety or boiler service records.

## Step 5: Present your flat well

- **Declutter** and **deep clean**.
- **Fix small repairs**: dripping taps, loose handles, marked walls.
- **Maximise light**: open blinds, clean windows, use lamps.
- **Tidy communal areas** where you can, or ask the managing agent.

Professional photography and an accurate floor plan make a big difference online, where most buyers first see your flat.

## Step 6: Marketing and viewings

Your agent will list your flat on property portals and contact buyers on their database. Listings must include **material information**, such as tenure, lease length, service charges, ground rent and council tax band, so buyers can make informed decisions.

Your agent should handle viewings, give honest feedback and keep you updated.

## Step 7: Receive and accept an offer

When an offer comes in, your agent should check that the buyer can proceed:

- **mortgage agreement in principle**
- **proof of deposit** or funds
- **chain position**: are they a first-time buyer, chain-free, or dependent on selling their own home?

The highest offer isn't always the best one. A chain-free buyer with a mortgage in place may be worth more to you than a higher offer from someone with a long chain.

## Step 8: Conveyancing

Once you've accepted an offer, both sides instruct solicitors or licensed conveyancers. Your solicitor will:

- prepare the draft contract
- answer the buyer's enquiries
- provide property information forms
- obtain the leasehold management pack.

The buyer's solicitor will carry out searches, and the buyer's lender will value the property. The buyer may also commission a survey.

For leasehold flats, conveyancing often takes longer than for freehold houses, typically several months from offer to completion.

## Step 9: Exchange of contracts

When all enquiries are answered and both sides are ready, solicitors **exchange contracts**. At this point the sale is legally binding and a completion date is fixed.

## Step 10: Completion

On completion day, the buyer's money is transferred, your mortgage is paid off, and you hand over the keys. Your agent will then invoice their fee, usually paid through your solicitor.

## How long does it take?

Every sale is different, but plan for several months from accepting an offer to completion for a leasehold flat. Preparing documents early is the single best way to speed things up.
