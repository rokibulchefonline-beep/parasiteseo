Every bin bag of food leaving your kitchen is money you've already spent: on ingredients, on energy to cook them and on the staff time to prepare them. In a business where margins are often thin, cutting food waste is one of the quickest ways to improve profit without raising a single price.

Here's where waste usually comes from, and what you can do about it.

## Where does the waste come from?

Most kitchen waste falls into three groups:

- **Spoilage**: stock that goes off before it's used, through over-ordering, poor rotation or incorrect storage.
- **Preparation waste**: trimmings, peelings, and over-prepped items that can't be held safely until the next service.
- **Plate and order waste**: food that's cooked but not eaten, from oversized portions, wrong orders or dishes that arrive cold and get thrown away.

You can't fix what you don't measure, so start there.

## Step 1: Measure for two weeks

Put three labelled bins or containers in the kitchen, one for each type of waste above. At the end of each service, weigh them or simply estimate how full they are, and jot it down. After a fortnight, patterns appear:

- Which ingredients are thrown away most?
- Which days produce the most prep waste?
- Which dishes come back half-eaten, or generate complaints?

This doesn't need special software. A clipboard on the wall is enough.

## Step 2: Order to match real demand

Over-ordering is the biggest cause of spoilage. Use your sales data to order more accurately:

- Look at **sales by day of the week**. Most takeaways sell very differently on a Tuesday and a Saturday.
- Factor in **events**: big football matches, bank holidays, Ramadan, Diwali, Christmas and school holidays.
- Order **fresh produce more often in smaller quantities** where your suppliers allow.
- Keep a **par level** (the amount you need on hand) for each key ingredient and adjust it monthly.

An EPoS that combines dine-in, phone and online orders makes this far easier, because all your sales data is in one place.

## Step 3: Store and rotate properly

- Follow **first in, first out (FIFO)**: new deliveries go to the back, older stock to the front.
- **Label and date** everything you prepare, using a simple day-dot or date-label system.
- Keep fridges at **5°C or below** and freezers at **-18°C or below**, and check them daily.
- Store raw meat below ready-to-eat foods to avoid cross-contamination, which also avoids having to throw away contaminated food.

These are good food safety practice too, and they help you towards a top food hygiene rating.

## Step 4: Prep smarter

Batch preparation is essential in a busy kitchen, but over-prepping is a common source of waste.

- **Prep to your forecast**, not to habit. If Tuesdays are quiet, prep less.
- **Prep in stages**: prepare a base quantity, then top up during service if needed.
- **Use trimmings**: vegetable offcuts can go into stocks and base sauces; bread can become croutons or crumbs.
- **Cross-use ingredients** across several dishes so nothing sits waiting for one rarely ordered item.

## Step 5: Review your menu

A long menu with many rarely ordered dishes means more ingredients sitting around. Look at your sales report:

- Which dishes sell only a handful of times a month?
- Do they need ingredients nothing else uses?

Removing or reworking slow sellers reduces stock, waste and kitchen complexity. Your remaining dishes are likely to be fresher and more consistent too.

## Step 6: Get portion sizes right

If plates regularly come back half full, portions may be too large. Consider:

- **standard portion scoops and ladles** so every portion is the same
- offering **half portions** or **lighter options**
- **asking customers** about portion sizes in feedback.

Consistent portions also make your food costs predictable, which helps with pricing.

## Step 7: Reduce order mistakes

A wrong order often means a whole meal thrown away and a second one cooked for free. Most errors come from:

- online orders re-typed by hand into the till
- unclear notes about spice levels or allergies
- missing items at the packing stage.

Sending online orders **straight to the kitchen printer** and using a **packing checklist** cuts errors sharply. Platforms such as [ChefOnline](https://www.chefonline.com/) connect online ordering with EPoS and kitchen printing, so orders aren't copied by hand.

## Step 8: Do something useful with surplus food

Some surplus is unavoidable. Rather than binning safe, unsold food:

- **Staff meals** are the obvious first use.
- **Food redistribution apps and charities** can collect surplus from businesses. Check local options and their food safety requirements.
- **Food waste collection**: separate food waste collection is being rolled out for businesses in England under the Simpler Recycling reforms, so check what applies to your premises and use it for genuine waste.

Always follow food safety rules: never give away food that has been held at unsafe temperatures.

## Step 9: Involve the whole team

Waste reduction works best when everyone understands why it matters. Share the numbers from your waste log, set a simple target, and celebrate when you hit it. Kitchen staff often have the best ideas about where waste happens.

## How much could you save?

Every kitchen is different, but the logic is simple: if you buy £2,000 of ingredients a week and throw away even 5% of them, that's £100 a week, or more than £5,000 a year, before you count the energy and labour that went into them. Even a modest reduction goes straight to your bottom line.

## The bottom line

Measure your waste, order to real demand, store and rotate properly, prep to forecast, trim the menu, standardise portions and cut order errors. None of these steps is complicated, and together they can save a takeaway thousands of pounds a year.
